Day 1 – Nov 20, 2026

Title: The Evolution of Biomolecular Intelligence: From Geometric Modeling to Autonomous Agents and Native Reasoning

Speaker: Wengong Jin

Affiliation: Assistant Professor, Northeastern University

Abstract: Learning from molecular graphs and three-dimensional structures has transformed our ability to predict molecular properties and design new molecules. In this talk, I will trace the progression of biomolecular intelligence through three stages. First, I will discuss geometric biomolecular intelligence, from breakthroughs such as AlphaFold and RFdiffusion to our work on Chemprop, JT-VAE, and FlashBind, highlighting applications to discovering novel antibiotics against drug-resistant bacteria. Second, as the growing diversity of molecular models creates new challenges in choosing objectives and developing effective workflows, I will introduce agentic biomolecular intelligence. Through SAGA and MolRSI, I will show how agents can adapt models and workflows to biological problems and improve their decisions through experience, with computational and experimental results in antibiotic discovery and protein design. Lastly, I will explore an emerging frontier: can the language models develop a direct understanding of biomolecular structure without tool orchestration? I will discuss our ongoing work toward native biomolecular intelligence, where structural understanding becomes an integral part of language-model reasoning.

Bio: Wengong Jin is an assistant professor at Khoury College of Computer Sciences at Northeastern University, with affiliation at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard and Mass General Hospital. His research focuses on geometric, generative, and agentic AI methods for drug discovery and biology. His work has been published in venues including ICML, NeurIPS, ICLR, Nature, Science, Cell, and PNAS, and covered by outlets such as the Guardian, BBC News, CBS Boston, and the Financial Times. He is the recipient of the Google Research Scholar Award, BroadIgnite Award, Dimitris N. Chorafas Prize, and MIT EECS Outstanding Thesis Award.

Day 2 – Nov 21, 2026

Title: Graph-based Learning for Accelerated Combinatorial Optimization

Speaker: Rajmonda Caceres

Affiliation: Senior Technical Staff, MIT Lincoln Lab

Abstract: Machine learning is increasingly used to accelerate complex combinatorial optimization problems, but in mission-critical settings, speed alone is insufficient: feasibility and performance guarantees remain essential. Pure neural approaches often fall short of classical methods, such as mixed-integer programming, in producing feasible, high-quality solutions. However, exploiting the underlying structure of optimization problems offers opportunities to address these limitations.

In this talk, we discuss recent work on multi-agent assignment, set cover, and dense-subgraph detection under partial observability, showing how graph-based abstractions shape the design of effective learning architectures, including graph neural networks and reinforcement learning. Across these settings, careful problem-to-graph abstraction, paired with hybrid learning-and-optimization design, enables learned methods to combine the speed and adaptability of modern machine learning with the feasibility and optimality guarantees required by mission-critical applications.

We discuss implications for hybrid classical-and-learned approaches to combinatorial optimization, in which learned components exploit the optimization problem structure to accelerate and guide traditional solvers rather than replace them.

Bio: Rajmonda Caceres is a Senior Technical Staff at MIT Lincoln Laboratory, where her research spans machine learning, network science, and robust algorithm design for complex systems. Her recent work focuses on graph neural networks for accelerated combinatorial optimization, as well as physics-informed machine learning.

Day 2 – Nov 21, 2026

Title: Geometric Deep Learning, in 2026 and beyond

Speaker: Petar Veličković

Affiliation: Senior Staff Research Scientist, Google Deepmind & University of Cambridge

Abstract: To accompany the recent release of the complete draft of the Geometric Deep Learning book, join me for a tale of symmetry, groups, and deep learning – with a frank look at why geometric priors remain essential to understanding AI in 2026, and a diverse overview of what lies beyond what GDL can express.

Bio: Petar is a Senior Staff Research Scientist at Google DeepMind, Affiliated Lecturer at the University of Cambridge, and an Associate of Clare Hall, Cambridge. He holds a PhD in Computer Science from the University of Cambridge (Trinity College), obtained under the supervision of Pietro Liò. His research concerns aligning neural networks to (classical) computation, to assess and improve their out-of-distribution generalisation ability. Particularly, he focuses on neural algorithmic reasoning (featured in VentureBeat), graph representation learning and categorical and geometric deep learning (a topic he has co-written a proto-book about). For his contributions, he is recognised as an ELLIS Scholar in the Geometric Deep Learning Program. He is the first author of Graph Attention Networks—a popular convolutional layer for graphs—and Deep Graph Infomax—a popular self-supervised learning pipeline for graphs (featured in ZDNet). His research has been used in substantially improving travel-time predictions in Google Maps (featured in Endgadget, VentureBeat, CNET, The Verge and ZDNet), guiding intuition of mathematicians towards new top-tier theorems and conjectures (featured in Nature, Science, Quanta Magazine, New Scientist, The Independent, Sky News, The Sunday Times, la Repubblica and The Conversation), the first full AI system for tactical suggestions in association football (featured in Financial Times, The Athletic, The Economist, New Scientist, Wired, MIT Technology Review, The Verge and El País), and the first AI-assisted top-percentile result in competitive programming.

Day 3 – Nov 22, 2026

Title: When Can AI Transfer Across Power Grids?

Speaker: Anna Scaglione

Affiliation: Stephen M. Ross/Related Companies Professor, Cornell Tech

Abstract: AI models for power systems are often trained for a particular network and set of operating conditions. But power grids change, and a model that works on one system may not work on another. How can we tell when what a model has learned will transfer?

In this talk, I will explore how the structure and physics of power networks can help answer that question. I will discuss representations that capture features of a network and its response, and how comparisons between systems can help assess whether a model trained on one grid is relevant to another. Drawing on recent work on distribution systems, I will consider both the opportunities and the limits of transfer, and what further evidence is needed before transferred models can be trusted in operation.

Bio: Anna Scaglione is the Stephen M. Ross/Related Companies Professor of Electrical and Computer Engineering at Cornell Tech and Cornell Engineering. Her research brings together statistical signal processing, network science, decentralized optimization, and machine learning, with applications to communication networks and energy infrastructure. She directs the Signal, Information, Networks and Energy laboratory, whose work includes graph learning and foundation models for the electric grid. She is an IEEE Fellow.
